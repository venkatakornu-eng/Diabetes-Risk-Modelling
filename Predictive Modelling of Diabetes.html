Aim and Objectives

The aim of this study is to analyse and predict the variables linked to adult diabetes using machine learning and statistical techniques. This entails identifying important biological and lifestyle predictors, assessing their relative significance, and using clustering techniques to investigate underlying patterns within the population.

To accomplish this goal, the subsequent objectives were established:

  • To ascertain critical biological and lifestyle determinants affecting diabetes through logistic regression and random forest methodologies.

  • To evaluate the persistence of lifestyle behaviours as significant predictors of diabetes after adjusting for biological variables, including age and BMI.

  • To use K-means clustering to find different groups in the population based on their health and lifestyle traits.

  • To compare the number of people with diabetes in different clusters and find the groups that are most at risk.

  • To assess the efficacy and comprehensibility of various modelling methodologies in forecasting diabetes outcomes.

Reasearch Questions

  • Which biological and lifestyle factors significantly predict the likelihood of diabetes among adults?

  • Do lifestyle behaviours remain significant predictors of diabetes after controlling for biological factors such as age and BMI?

  • Are there distinct clusters of individuals based on health and lifestyle characteristics, and how do these clusters differ in diabetes prevalence?

Reflective Analysis

This Project applied multiple modeling techniques to analyse diabetes risk, each with its own set of strengths. Logistic regression made it easy to understand important predictors like BMI and age. Random forest, on the other hand, made predictions more accurate by finding more complicated relationships. The decision tree made it easier to understand by using simple decision rules, but it was less stable.

K-means clustering helped find separate groups in the data, showing how diabetes risk varies between different groups of people. However, clustering necessitated meticulous selection of variables and the quantity of clusters.

Class imbalance was a major problem with all of the models, which made it hard to accurately predict diabetes cases. Adding socioeconomic and lifestyle factors also raises ethical and EDI issues because these factors could introduce bias.

In general, using a combination of different methods gave us a better picture of diabetes risk by balancing how well they predicted and how easy they were to understand.

Results

The results from logistic regression and random forest models indicate that BMI and age are the strongest predictors of diabetes. Diabetes risk is consistently linked to higher BMI and older age groups. Healthy habits are associated with a lower risk, and lifestyle factors like diet and exercise also play a role. Socioeconomic variables like income and education also have a big impact; higher levels are typically linked to a lower prevalence of diabetes. In general, both lifestyle and biological factors are important, but biological factors are more significant.

K-means clustering was applied using selected variables including BMI, age, income, physical activity, and dietary habitsTo facilitate clustering, factor encoding was used to transform categorical variables into numerical form. Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate how important biological, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors are in determining the risk of diabetes. Age and BMI were consistently found to be the best predictors in every model, demonstrating their significant influence on diabetes outcomes [1]. Diabetes risk was also linked to lifestyle factors like diet and physical activity, but these effects diminished when age and BMI were taken into account, indicating that biological factors may be more significant [2]. The prevalence of diabetes was clearly correlated with socioeconomic status, especially income, with lower risk associated with higher income levels [3]. The notion that risk is not evenly distributed was further supported by clustering analysis, which identified distinct groups within the population, with one cluster showing a higher proportion of diabetes cases [4].Class imbalance made it difficult for the models to accurately predict minority classes, despite their high accuracy [5]. All things considered, the findings offer insightful information about the intricate interactions between variables that lead to diabetes and emphasize the necessity of focused interventions.

Furthermore, the trade-off between interpretability and predictive performance is highlighted by comparing various modeling approaches. Logistic regression is helpful for comprehending relationships within the data because it gave clear insights into the significance of individual variables. By capturing intricate, non-linear interactions, the random forest model, on the other hand, attained marginally higher accuracy at the expense of decreased interpretability. Although it was less stable and more likely to be biased in favor of the majority class, the decision tree provided a balance by graphically representing decision rules. These variations highlight how crucial it is to choose the right models based on the goal of the study [7]. Combining interpretable and high-performing models can yield meaningful insights for decision-making as well as accurate predictions for healthcare applications [6].
